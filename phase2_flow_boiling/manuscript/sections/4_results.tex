\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Forward accuracy}
\label{sec:forward}

Table~\ref{tab:metrics} compares the model with the five classical correlations
on the held-out test split. The PINN attains a $\dTonb$ RMSE of \SI{1.84}{K}
($R^2=0.77$) and a $\qonb$ RMSE of \SI{144}{kW\,m^{-2}} ($R^2=0.74$). The best
classical correlation (Bergles--Rohsenow) reaches \SI{3.73}{K} on $\dTonb$, and
all correlations have strongly negative $R^2$ on $\qonb$---i.e.\ worse than
predicting the mean---because they were not built for this combined parameter
range. The PINN is thus about $1.9$--$2.6\times$ more accurate on $\dTonb$ and
more than an order of magnitude more accurate on $\qonb$. Figure~\ref{fig:parity}
shows the test parity on logarithmic axes: predictions lie within the
$\pm20\%$ band across the full $2$--\SI{30}{K} and $30$--\SI{1500}{kW\,m^{-2}}
ranges, including the low-superheat microchannel and high-pressure points.

\subsection{The role of transfer learning and data curation}

Accuracy improved monotonically as the dataset was cleaned and extended and as
two physical fixes were applied (Section~\ref{sec:physcon}). Removing
pool-boiling contamination (Section~\ref{sec:data}) was the single largest
gain, cutting the $\dTonb$ RMSE from \SI{4.0}{K} to \SI{1.85}{K}; adding the
microchannel data sharpened $\qonb$ ($R^2$ from $0.54$ to $0.78$); and the
high-pressure anchors with the reduced-pressure feature lowered $\dTonb$ RMSE to
\SI{1.84}{K} while fixing the pressure trend. The frozen pool-boiling surface
encoder supplies the surface representation at no additional flow-data cost,
consistent with the transfer-learning premise of the study.

\subsection{Physics consistency}
\label{sec:physcon}

A predictor intended for design must respect the established physical trends.
We verify the sign of $\partial\dTonb/\partial\xi$ for each governing variable.

\subsubsection{Subcooling--Hsu conflict (low superheat)}
\label{sec:patternB}
An early model systematically under-predicted $\dTonb$ in the low-superheat
($<\!\SI{5}{K}$), high-subcooling regime. The cause was the Hsu
\emph{equality} coupling: at the low heat fluxes of these points the
saturated-pool Hsu superheat is near zero ($\sim\!\SI{0.06}{K}$), and the
equality term dragged predictions down against measured values of
$2$--\SI{5}{K}. Replacing it with the one-sided lower bound of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:onesided} removed the bias: on the most affected source the test
error became unbiased (mean error $+\SI{0.03}{K}$, RMSE \SI{0.62}{K}). This
confirms that the saturated-pool criterion should bound, not equal, the
subcooled-flow superheat.

\subsubsection{Pressure dependence}
\label{sec:patternC}
Pressure has a first-order effect ($P\!\uparrow\!\Rightarrow\!\dTonb\!\downarrow$,
the Bergles--Rohsenow trend), yet a model in which pressure entered only through
fluid properties was \emph{blind} to it: the correlation between pressure and
predicted superheat was $+0.01$ against a measured $-0.14$ over the
high-pressure subset. Adding reduced pressure as an explicit input feature
flipped the sign of the predicted correlation, and adding the
\SI{10}{}--\SI{16}{MPa} anchors strengthened it to $-0.69$ (measured $-0.57$
over the combined high-pressure data); the model then tracks the Wang et al.\
pressure sweep in the correct descending direction. Property-based
nondimensionalization alone is therefore insufficient to encode the pressure
dependence of incipience; an explicit reduced-pressure input is required.

\subsubsection{Remaining trends}
On representative sweeps the model satisfies $\dTsub\!\uparrow\!\Rightarrow\!
\dTonb\!\uparrow$ and, through the Hsu coupling and the data,
$q\!\uparrow\!\Rightarrow\!\dTonb\!\uparrow$ (in-source correlations
$+0.57$ to $+0.88$). The Reynolds- and mass-flux sensitivities are weak and
nearly flat at fixed pressure, consistent with experimental reports that ONB
superheat depends only mildly on mass flux in this regime.

\subsection{Uncertainty quantification}
\label{sec:uq}

A five-member deep ensemble (identical data split, varied initialisation)
provides predictive uncertainty. The ensemble mean is also \emph{more accurate}
than any single model ($\dTonb$ RMSE \SI{1.72}{K}, $R^2=0.80$;
Table~\ref{tab:metrics} single-model value \SI{1.84}{K}), a known benefit of
ensembling. Figure~\ref{fig:uq} shows the ensemble parity with $\pm2\sigma$
epistemic bars, the calibration curve, and the epistemic spread versus pressure.

The calibration analysis is deliberately honest. The mean epistemic standard
deviation is \SI{0.51}{K}, and the $1\sigma$/$2\sigma$ coverages are only
$28\%$/$42\%$ (ideal $68\%$/$95\%$): the ensemble \emph{under}-estimates total
predictive uncertainty by roughly $4.7\times$. This is expected---deep-ensemble
spread captures only epistemic (model) disagreement, whereas the dominant
uncertainty here is aleatoric: digitisation error and genuine experimental
scatter of order \SI{1}{K}. A single scaling factor of $4.7$ yields
well-calibrated intervals as an interim measure (green curve,
Fig.~\ref{fig:uq}b); a heteroscedastic variance head, which would learn the
aleatoric component directly, is identified as the proper remedy and left to
future work. Notably the epistemic spread does \emph{not} flag the
high-pressure region as uncertain (the members agree there, anchored by the
Wang et al.\ data), confirming that the residual error there is
aleatoric/structural rather than a lack of model consensus.
