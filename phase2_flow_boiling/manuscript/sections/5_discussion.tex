\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Engineering implications}

Across the assembled range the model halves the wall-superheat error of the
best classical correlation (\SI{1.84}{K} versus \SI{3.7}{K}) and is more than an
order of magnitude better on heat flux. For design this translates into a
tighter incipience margin: a thermal-margin uncertainty of $\sim\!\SI{4}{K}$
typical of correlation scatter is reduced to $\sim\!\SI{2}{K}$, allowing less
conservative operating points in microchannel electronics cooling and a
sharper boiling-incipience boundary in compact refrigerant evaporators. Because
a single model spans $\Dh = 0.35$--\SI{9.5}{mm} and $P=0.1$--\SI{16}{MPa}, the
same predictor serves applications previously requiring different correlations,
from atmospheric microchannels to high-pressure nuclear channels. As a concrete
artefact, Fig.~\ref{fig:designmap} renders the model as a design map: predicted
$\dTonb$ over the mass-flux--pressure plane for a representative stainless
channel, directly usable to place an operating point relative to incipience. The
map encodes the learned physics---superheat falling with pressure and rising
with mass flux at low pressure---in a single continuous surface. The
pressure-dependent mass-flux sensitivity is itself physically consistent: a
higher mass flux thins the thermal boundary layer and so raises the superheat
required for a cavity to reach the nucleation condition, but the magnitude of
that effect scales with the intrinsic incipience superheat
($\propto\sigma\Tsat/\rhov\hfg$), which is large at low pressure and small at
high pressure. The convective thinning therefore produces a several-kelvin swing
near atmospheric pressure but only a faint one in the high-pressure, dense-vapour
limit, exactly the trend the design map displays.

\subsection{Why an explicit reduced-pressure feature matters}

The pressure result (Section~\ref{sec:patternC}) is methodologically general.
It is tempting to assume that nondimensionalising targets and inputs with
pressure-dependent fluid properties lets a network ``see'' pressure implicitly.
We find this is not so for incipience: the property variations enter the
Reynolds, Weber and Boiling numbers too weakly for the network to recover the
first-order $P\!\uparrow\!\Rightarrow\!\dTonb\!\downarrow$ trend, and the model
remained pressure-blind until reduced pressure was supplied as a direct input.
Practitioners building data-driven boiling models should include pressure (or
reduced pressure) explicitly rather than relying on property-based
nondimensionalization to encode it.

\subsection{Physics-regularized regression versus full PINN}

The model is a physics-regularized regressor, not a field-resolving PINN. This
is a deliberate choice for sparse, heterogeneous incipience data: the scalar
ONB targets are what experiments report and what design needs, and soft Hsu and
monotonicity constraints inject the relevant physics without the data demands
and ill-posedness of full Navier--Stokes residuals on a scalar problem. Our
attempt to add a one-dimensional energy residual on an auxiliary temperature
field confirmed the trade-off: it regularised only an unsupervised field and
destabilised optimisation, with no accuracy benefit. Full field resolution
remains attractive where dense field data or trustworthy CFD are available, and
is a natural Phase~3 direction.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations bound the present claims. First and most important, the
headline accuracy reflects \emph{interpolation within the sampled studies}:
under leave-one-study-out cross-validation (Section~\ref{sec:loso},
Table~\ref{tab:loso}) the wall-superheat error rises to $5.7$--\SI{10.9}{K}, so
the model should be applied for interpolation within the trained envelope rather
than extrapolated to an entirely unseen study, fluid or geometry. Second, the
dataset, though wide in range, is modest in size (351 points) and dominated by
water; refrigerant and non-aqueous coverage is thin, and the high-pressure end
rests on a single study, so behaviour beyond \SI{16}{MPa} is unverified. Third,
surface descriptors are sparse in the flow-boiling literature---most records
lack measured roughness and contact angle---so the transferred surface encoder
acts mainly as a frozen prior (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) and is exercised less
here than in pool boiling. Fourth, the uncertainty quantification captures
epistemic but not aleatoric uncertainty, under-covering by $\sim\!4.7\times$, so
calibrated intervals presently require the interim rescaling. We tested the
obvious remedy---a heteroscedastic variance head trained by Gaussian negative
log-likelihood---but at this data scale it did not help: $1\sigma$ coverage
stayed at $28\%$ and accuracy fell to \SI{2.13}{K}, as $\sim\!240$ heterogeneous
points are too few to learn a reliable per-point variance. A calibrated
aleatoric model therefore awaits a substantially larger dataset rather than a
richer output head.
Fifth, the flow-aware Hsu constraint sets the thermal-boundary-layer thickness
through a Nusselt correlation; the main results use the turbulent Dittus--Boelter
form, which is not strictly appropriate for the low-Reynolds microchannel subset.
We therefore re-trained with a physically preferable regime-dependent closure
(laminar $Nu=4.36$, Gnielinski transition, Dittus--Boelter turbulent) and found
the test wall-superheat RMSE essentially unchanged, in fact marginally improved
(\SI{1.78}{K} vs \SI{1.84}{K}); the constraint is thus robust to this choice.
Finally, a narrow intermediate-pressure, low-mass-flux pocket (a single source's
$3.5$--\SI{5}{MPa} runs) retains a residual over-prediction of
$\sim\!\SI{1.7}{K}$ that reflects a data gap rather than a model defect.
